Kit alternatives: five options, and when each one wins

Most pages ranking for this query are published by one of the alternatives, which makes the conclusion easy to predict. Here is the version without a horse in the race: five platforms, current pricing read from their own pages today, and the specific situation in which each one beats Kit — including the situations where nothing beats Kit.

The short answer. Leaving Kit over price → MailerLite or EmailOctopus. Leaving because the list got big and expensive → beehiiv. Leaving because you want to sell courses and funnels in one place → Systeme.io. Leaving because you want zero fixed cost → Substack. Leaving because automations are hard → you are probably better off staying.

What Kit costs, so there is a baseline

Kit's free Newsletter plan runs to 10,000 subscribers with one basic visual automation. The Creator plan is $39/month billed monthly, or $390/year at 1,000 subscribers, and unlocks unlimited automations. Selling anything through Kit costs 3.5% + 30¢ per transaction on every plan, including the paid ones. That last number is the one people usually discover late.

1. MailerLite — the cheap, competent one

MailerLite is the alternative most people actually mean when they say Kit is expensive. Comfort starts at $12/month against Kit's $39, and it stays cheaper at every tier we compared. You get 50 automations, 10 landing pages, 10 websites and 5 digital products on that plan, plus a drag-and-drop editor that is more pleasant than Kit's.

The catch: the free plan is 250 subscribers and 2,500 emails a month. If you are on Kit's free plan with 4,000 subscribers, moving to MailerLite means starting to pay immediately. That is the opposite of a saving.

Move if: you are already on Kit's Creator plan and the automation depth is not being used. Do not move if: you are still inside Kit's free tier.

2. beehiiv — the one that wins on a big list

beehiiv's Scale plan is $43/month and covers up to 100,000 subscribers. Kit's price climbs with subscriber count. Somewhere above roughly 10,000 subscribers that difference stops being a rounding error and starts being the main line in your costs.

beehiiv also monetizes differently: an ad network that places sponsors into your newsletter, paid subscriptions at a 0% platform take rate, referral mechanics and Boosts. If the newsletter is the product rather than the shop window, this is the better-shaped tool.

The catch: the free plan has no automations whatsoever, and 2,500 subscribers is a quarter of Kit's free ceiling. beehiiv wants you paying before you have revenue.

beehiiv pricing — not an affiliate link. Full breakdown in Kit vs beehiiv.

3. EmailOctopus — the cheapest way to keep sending

EmailOctopus gives you 2,500 subscribers and 10,000 emails a month free, with EmailOctopus branding on the emails and one landing page. Pro starts at $9/month billed yearly and removes the branding.

This is the right answer surprisingly often. A large share of people paying for creator email software use campaigns, one signup form and nothing else. If that is you, most of what Kit charges for is inventory you are not using.

The catch: no commerce, thin automation, and a free plan that puts someone else's brand at the bottom of your emails.

EmailOctopus pricing — not an affiliate link.

4. Substack — no monthly cost, a percentage instead

Substack charges no platform fee and no per-subscriber fee. It takes 10% of paid subscription revenue, and Stripe takes its processing cut on top (2.9% + 30¢ per transaction, plus a recurring billing fee). If nobody pays you, Substack costs nothing at all.

The arithmetic flips as you grow. 10% of a $50,000/year paid newsletter is $5,000 — several times what any of the platforms above would charge for the same list. Substack is cheap while you are small and expensive once you are not.

The catch: it is a publishing network, not an email tool. Automation, tagging, product delivery and list portability are all weaker, and the network effects that help you find readers are the same ones that keep you inside the network.

Substack — not an affiliate link. Substack has no affiliate programme, which is exactly why it is under-recommended on pages like this one.

5. Systeme.io — email plus funnels plus courses, free

Systeme.io bundles email with sales funnels, a course platform, a blog and an affiliate programme. The free plan carries 2,000 contacts, unlimited email sends, 3 funnels and 1 course, and there are no transaction fees on sales at any tier. Startup is $17/month for 5,000 contacts.

Against Kit's 3.5% + 30¢ per sale, 0% transaction fees is the headline — but do the arithmetic before you move for it. Kit's fee includes card processing, and Systeme.io still sends you to Stripe at roughly 2.9% + 30¢, so Kit's own margin is about 0.6%. That is around $12/month on $2,000 of sales and $60/month on $10,000. Real, but smaller than the headline suggests. The full fee arithmetic is in the digital products guide.

The catch: it is a jack-of-all-trades. Each individual piece is less polished than the specialist tool, the email editor is basic, and the deliverability reputation of an all-in-one platform is harder to reason about than a dedicated sender's.

When you should stay on Kit

  • You are under 10,000 subscribers and not paying yet. No alternative on this page gives you that for free. Leaving costs money.
  • You run genuine behavioural automations. Tag-and-trigger logic is the thing Kit is actually best at, and rebuilding it elsewhere takes longer than the saving is worth.
  • Your revenue is products sold to the list. Commerce is on every Kit plan including free, and the fee only bites once sales are real.
  • You are considering the move because of one annoying feature. Migration costs deliverability, links, forms and a weekend. Make sure the reason is structural.

How to leave without breaking things

  1. Export first. Take the subscriber list, the tags and the sequence copy before you cancel anything.
  2. Warm up. Do not send to 8,000 people on day one from a new platform. Send to your most engaged segment first.
  3. Keep the forms live. Repoint embedded forms before you turn off the old account, not after.
  4. Check the automations one by one. Sequences almost never import cleanly. Rebuild the two that matter and delete the rest — most of them were not running anyway.
